## A compromise, wearing the shape of a keyboard

The DOS picture is 4:3. A phone in landscape is 20:9. The difference is two black bars, one on each side of the picture, and for two releases those bars were where the keys lived.

They were not bad bars. The arrows went into the inverted T of a real keyboard, the modifiers and the editing keys into the other side, and the picture kept the whole middle of the screen. It worked.

It was also the wrong shape for the thing it was standing in for. A DOS game is played with the left thumb on the arrows and the right thumb on the keys, and every one of those keys was a long way from where a thumb expects it.

## What a handheld console does

A handheld console puts the screen in the middle and the controls in your hands — one hand on each side — and it never lets the controls sit on top of the picture.

That is the whole idea behind 1.0.0.9. The bars are no longer two columns of whatever fitted; they are **two pads**, and the picture sits untouched in the middle.

The **left pad** is the thumb that steers: the four arrows as a **D-pad** — up on top, left and right side by side, down below, so the finger reads the direction by shape alone — and above them the left half of a split QWERTY, with **File**, **Esc** and **Tab** along the top.

The **right pad** is the thumb that acts: **Alt**, **Ctrl**, **Shift**, **Space** and **Enter** under the right thumb, the right half of the QWERTY above them, and **#+=**, **Fn** and Backspace along the top.

The QWERTY is split rather than shrunk. `12345 qwert asdfg zxcvb` on the left, `67890 yuiop hjkl: nm./\` on the right — each half is a real key size, not a miniature of the whole thing.

## Three layers, one key each

A split keyboard is only half the story; the other half is what you do when you run out of keys. The answer here is **one key per layer**, in the top row of the right pad.

**#+=** swaps both halves for the punctuation. The key lights up blue while its layer is on, and turns into *abc*, because that is what pressing it brings you back to.

And because the letter layer of the right half already carries `: . / \`, typing a DOS path needs no layer switch at all: `C:\GAMES\DUNE\SETUP.EXE` is just letters and punctuation, in that order, on one layer.

## Ctrl, Alt and Shift, the way a game wants them

The modifiers were the part I had to think about longest, because two different games want two different things from Ctrl.

A *tap* on Ctrl, Alt or Shift **arms** it: the next key you press is modified, and the modifier releases itself. Ctrl, then C — that is what a text editor or a DOS shell wants.

A *hold* keeps it pressed for as long as your finger is down, like a real key. That is what a game wants from its fire and strafe keys: hold Ctrl on the right thumb while the left thumb walks the D-pad, and the game sees both at once.

The old keyboard could only do the first one. The new pads do both, because a modifier is just a key that is down.

## The layout follows the screen

Not every phone has 20:9 of letterbox to work with, so the keyboard picks its shape from the width of the bar: the full pads when there is room for them (at least 116 dp), a single key column per side with the fold-up QWERTY when there is less, and a slim strip along the bottom in portrait.

On a 20:9 screen the letter keys come out at **32.8 dp** wide. On an 18:9 screen — the shape of a lot of phones — the bars are narrower and the same layout leaves the letters at **23.6 dp**, about 2.9 % of the screen width, where a normal phone keyboard key is roughly twice that. The D-pad and the action keys keep their full size; it is the letters that get tight.

I measured it rather than guessed: both widths were run on the emulator and typed into, and the screenshots of all three layers at both widths are in the repository. What I have *not* done is try it on a physical 18:9 phone, and that is the gap I would want a reader with such a phone to close for me.

## Nothing underneath changed

The whole release is app-layer work. The SDL surface stays full-screen, the keyboard only ever draws in the bars beside the picture, and **`libdosbox.so` is the same library 1.0.0.8 shipped** — byte for byte the same native core, recompiled with the same flags.

Sometimes the honest way to describe a big change is to say what did *not* change.

## The pipeline builds the releases now

The repository has a CI pipeline for the first time. On every push and every pull request, `ci.yml` builds the emulator from source for all three ABIs — SDL2 and the whole DOSBox core, three times — and packages an app on top of them, checking that all three native libraries really made it into the APK.

On a `v*` tag, `release.yml` builds the release APK, signs it with the release key, checks the certificate against the known fingerprint, and publishes it as a GitHub Release with its SHA-256.

The key itself lives in the repository's secrets — the keystore base64-encoded, the store password and the alias beside it — and never in the tree. The pipeline refuses to publish if the certificate is not the one every previous release was signed with, because an APK signed with a different key cannot be installed over the old one, and finding that out on a user's phone is not a good place to find it.

## Thank you, DOSBox

None of this is possible without the DOSBox project.

**DOSBox** was written by the [DOSBox Team](https://www.dosbox.com/crew.php) — a volunteer crew who took an operating system from 1994 and made it runnable on every machine the world has produced since, including the ones that did not exist yet. They kept it alive for three decades and gave it away to anyone who wanted it. WhereQ-DOSBox is a thin layer on top of their work. The originality, the craft and the generosity are entirely theirs.

This port is built on **[Em-DOSBox](https://github.com/dreamlayers/em-dosbox)**, the Emscripten build of DOSBox r4305 by [Boris Gjenero](https://github.com/dreamlayers), which grew out of [Ismail Khatib's](https://github.com/CeRiAl) first attempt to compile DOSBox with Emscripten, and rode on the Emscripten work of [Alon Zakai](https://github.com/kripken/). The SDL2 library by [Sam Lantinga](https://github.com/libsdl-org/SDL) carries the window, the audio and the touch input.
