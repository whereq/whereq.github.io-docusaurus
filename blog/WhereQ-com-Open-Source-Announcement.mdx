## A pin is a very small sentence

Stand on a street in Toronto and look at the address in front of you. A map
gives you one claim about it: *there is a pin here*. A coffee shop. A hospital.
A school. Maybe a parking garage.

But the place itself is a pile of claims, and the pin is the least interesting
one of them.

Which school catchment is that address in? Can I walk to a library, and which
one? Is the fuel station on the corner charging $1.42 or $1.79? Is that
building inside a ward, or inside a neighbourhood, or in a ward *and* a
neighbourhood? Did anything happen on this street last week?

<!-- truncate -->

Every one of those answers is a piece of data, and every one of them lives
somewhere else. The school boundaries are in one system, the fuel prices in
another, the administrative outlines in a third, the local news in a fourth.
The map you are looking at is the *result* of all of them agreeing.

## So I built the part that agrees

The thing that surprised me while building it was how much of the work was not
the map at all.

Drawing pins is the easy half. The hard half is that a map is a *join*. Every
layer needs its own key, its own licence, its own rate limit, its own idea of
what a place is — and a POI (a point at an address) is not the same thing as
an administrative area (a polygon that contains an address), no matter how
casually you can slide one into the other.

So the first version of this app had three different ingestion scripts, each
one written for its own dataset, each one holding its own API key, each one
breaking on its own schedule.

That is the shape of the problem, and it is a shape almost every application
in the world has: *ten apps, ten scrapers, ten nightly failures*.

The fix was to make the retrieval a **layer of its own** — one process, one
cadence, one place where the outside world is allowed to enter the app.

I call it the crawler, and it is the part of this repository I am most pleased
with.

## The crawler

Everything on the map arrives by being crawled:

- **whereq.cloud** — fuel stations and their live prices, the aggregated price
  series, and the administrative geography: provinces, regions, municipalities,
  cities, towns, wards and neighbourhoods, with their real outlines.
- **OpenStreetMap** — the places around you. Coffee, food, parking, pharmacies,
  walk-in clinics, plazas, libraries.
- **RSS and JSON feeds** — the local news, events, stories and topics that you
  can switch on in the top-right corner of the map.

One account, one key, one rate limit. The whereq.cloud API allows 30 requests
a minute, so the two whereq.cloud crawls share a single throttle — two
independent limiters would each allow 30/min and would throttle each other
into 429s. It rotates through *coverage targets* round-robin (a country, a
province, a city, a map viewport) so a single tick never asks the upstream for
a whole continent, and a target that failed is retried on the next tick rather
than waiting out its interval.

The content crawler is the same idea with a parser attached: it discovers
items from the registered sources, normalizes them, extracts the location,
geocodes it through a provider chain, classifies them, and upserts them. A
feed that goes dark records `last_status='error'` and the crawl carries on.

**whereq.com** is a map-centric location platform. Open it and you see the
places around you; open any one of them and you get a proper page — address,
hours, categories, photos, the route to it, and the notes people left.

Some of what it does today:

- **Places and nearby** — a POI map, plus a nearby search that merges
  everything within a radius into one ranked list.
- **Place cards** — a full page per place, written by people, not by a feed.
- **Schools** — every school in the coverage area with its catchment boundary,
  drawn on the map.
- **Hazards** — environmental and infrastructure hazards, mapped and reported.
- **Fuel** — stations and live prices, crawled from whereq.cloud.
- **Geography** — the areas that *contain* the places, with real outlines.
- **Routes, friends, saved places, notifications** — the ordinary social parts
  of a map app.
- **English, French and Simplified Chinese**, throughout.

### The honest technical note

- **Frontend** — React 18, TypeScript, Vite, Tailwind. The typecheck is
  strict (`noUnusedLocals`, `noUnusedParameters`), and it is the only real
  gate the frontend has.
- **Backend** — FastAPI, SQLAlchemy, Pydantic, 18 Alembic migrations.
- **Database** — PostgreSQL 17 with PostGIS. Real geometry columns, a GiST
  index, and point-in-polygon tests that are not fooled by a simplified edge.
- **Licence** — MIT, for the whole repository, including the crawler.
- **Tests** — 235 of them, and they need no database and no network. The
  handful that want a live Postgres are opt-in.

**This post is not the documentation.** The documentation is the repository:
the setup guide, the crawler guide, the schema, the data sources and their
licences. I would rather you read those than take my word for it.

👉 **[github.com/whereq/WhereQ-com](https://github.com/whereq/WhereQ-com)**

To run it:

```sh
git clone https://github.com/whereq/WhereQ-com.git
cd WhereQ-com
cp docker/.env.example docker/.env
# set POSTGRES_PASSWORD and SECRET_KEY
docker compose -f docker/docker-compose.yml up -d --build
```

That is the whole stack — PostgreSQL with PostGIS, the migrations, the API, the
crawler and the nginx-served frontend. It comes up on `localhost:3743`.

**You do not need any keys to build, test or run it.** Leave the OIDC provider
empty and it runs in anonymous mode with the sign-in control hidden. Leave the
whereq.cloud key empty and the fuel panels stay empty and the geography feed
falls back to its public tier. The Google Maps key only decides whether the
map itself draws.

## Why it is in the world now

The repository has **one commit**.

That is not an accident, and it is not a fresh start from a template. I rewrote
the history before publishing it, because the history of this app contained
things that were never meant to leave my machine — credential literals, an
experiment with school data whose licence forbids redistribution, notes to
myself about a deployment. A git history is a permanent public record, and
deleting a line after the fact does not un-burn it.

So the history was rewritten, the private things were purged from every
commit, and what is published is the code, the migrations, the CI and the
documentation. The old commits do not exist anywhere a reader can reach them.

Beyond that, the honest reason is simpler: this map is the piece the rest of
the universe reads. It already serves a keyed geocoding API — geocode,
autocomplete, reverse geocode, routes, matrix, static maps — so sibling
applications can geocode without holding a Google key of their own. If that
service is going to be depended on by other people, then the crawler behind
it, and the map itself, should be readable by anyone who wants to run their
own.

If you want to run your own map of your own city, this is a starting point,
not a finished product. Take it.
